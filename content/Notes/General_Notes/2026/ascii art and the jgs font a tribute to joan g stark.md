Velvetyne published Jgs Font — a typeface by ASCII artist Adel Faure, created as a tribute to Joan G. Stark (jgs/Spunk), the most prolific ASCII artist of the 90s and 00s. The article is a deep dive into ASCII art history, the different textmode scenes, and what it means today.

Joan G. Stark started making ASCII art in 1995 on the alt.ascii-art Usenet newsgroup. She defined ASCII as "non-graphical graphics" — pictures made from characters on your keyboard. Her website ascii-art.com (archived on GeoCities) was the definitive collection of line-style drawings — mythological creatures, animals, everyday scenes — all rendered in keyboard characters. A self-taught folk artist working in the medium of text.

The piece traces the whole textmode art ecosystem: PETSCII (Commodore PET, used for games), ANSI (BBS scene, pirate groups trading art for access), ATASCII (Atari), SHIFT-JIS (Japanese 2chan characters like Kuma/Pedobear and Domo), Teletext (Prestel, Minitel). Each system had its own character set and scene — with groups, artists, publishing platforms like asciiarena.se, 16colo.rs, csdb.dk.

The article connects ASCII art to older traditions: printers in the Middle East used typesetting ornaments to mimic illuminated manuscripts. Unicode eventually absorbed all these encoding systems, but the scenes keep the old standards alive through emulation. The term "textmode art" has come to encompass the broader practice — not tied to any particular encoding system.

**Why it matters:** ASCII art sits at the intersection of constraint and creativity — 128 characters, a fixed grid, infinite possibility. It's also a rare case where the limitations of old hardware became an aesthetic rather than a bug to overcome. The Jgs Font formalizes Stark's line-style into a proper typeface, letting her visual language survive beyond the specific characters she drew with.
