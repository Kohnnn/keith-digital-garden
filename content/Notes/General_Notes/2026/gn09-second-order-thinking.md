# gn09 second-order thinking

First-order thinking is easy: "I want lower inflation, so raise rates." Second-order thinking asks the follow-up: "If rates rise, what happens to housing? To corporate borrowing? To consumer spending? To employment? To the next election cycle?" Most policy failures come from stopping at first-order consequences.

## key idea
Every action has multiple layers of consequence. The first order is usually obvious. The second and third orders are where the real outcomes live — and they often contradict the first order.

## examples
- Raising rates (first order) lowers inflation (second order) but increases unemployment (third order), which reduces tax revenue (fourth order), which forces spending cuts (fifth order)
- A hard fork in blockchain code (first order) creates two communities (second order), which compete for hashrate (third order), which can lead to chain reorganization risk (fourth order)
- Writing notes in public (first order) creates accountability (second order), which changes your reasoning process (third order), which compounds over years (fourth order)
