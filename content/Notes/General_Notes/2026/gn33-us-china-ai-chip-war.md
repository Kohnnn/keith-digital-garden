# The US-China AI Chip War in 2026: Export Controls and the Great Decoupling

The US-China chip war entered a decisive new phase in 2026. The BIS tightened controls from a presumption of denial to case-by-case review — but the unintended consequences are reshaping the global semiconductor landscape.

---

## The New Controls

On January 15, 2026, the BIS published a final rule tightening export controls on advanced semiconductors to China and Macau. Key change: the H200 and equivalents moved from "presumption of denial" to "case-by-case review," but with D:5 entities (Chinese-headquartered companies worldwide) now explicitly covered. The rules extend extraterritorially — any entity with Chinese headquarters, regardless of where they operate.

## The Unintended Consequence

NVIDIA lost its AI chip edge in China. The controls initially stalled H200 sales. By the time the Trump administration granted a reprieve, Beijing had already switched to domestic chips led by Huawei. Chinese AI companies now train on Huawei Ascend processors, not NVIDIA. The switch is structural — once the software stack was ported, there's no reason to go back.

## China's Response

Domestic chip production accelerated. Huawei's Ascend line now powers most Chinese AI training. SMIC improved yield on 7nm-class processes. Government subsidies shifted from "purchase domestic" to "design domestic" — engineering talent redirected to local chip architecture.

## The Fragmentation Risk

The global AI chip market is splitting into two ecosystems: NVIDIA/CUDA for the West, Huawei/Ascend for China. Software portability between them is non-trivial. Framework developers now maintain two backends. This is the AI equivalent of iOS vs Android — but in hardware.

The long-term risk: dual R&D costs for everyone. Chinese companies can't access CUDA-optimized models; Western companies lose access to Chinese AI research that runs on Ascend.
