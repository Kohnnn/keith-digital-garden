# EU AI Act Goes Live: What the August 2 Deadline Actually Means

The EU AI Act's first major enforcement wave hits August 2, 2026. Fines up to €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover. Extraterritorial scope — applies to any company whose AI outputs affect EU residents.

---

## The Four Risk Classes

The Act creates a four-tier framework:

**Unacceptable risk** — Banned outright. Social scoring, manipulative AI, real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces. Fines up to €35M or 7% of global turnover.

**High risk** — Full compliance regime. Conformity assessments, risk management systems, human oversight, transparency obligations. Covers: critical infrastructure, education, employment, law enforcement, migration, access to essential services.

**Limited risk** — Transparency obligations. Chatbots must disclose they're AI. Deepfakes must be labeled. Users must know they're interacting with AI.

**Minimal risk** — No obligations. Spam filters, AI-enabled video games.

## GPAI Rules (Articles 53-55)

General Purpose AI models face separate obligations: model documentation, training data summaries, copyright policy. Systemic risk GPAIs (FLOP threshold) require red-teaming, incident reporting, and cybersecurity protections.

## Who Gets Hit

The extraterritorial reach catches all frontier labs — OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta, Google — even if they have no EU office, as long as EU users can access their outputs. DeepSeek and other Chinese models face the same requirements if EU users interact with them.

The compliance cost is non-trivial: conformity assessments run €500K-2M per high-risk system. For a company with 50 high-risk deployments, that's real money. But non-compliance is worse — 7% of global turnover for the worst violations.

## The Reality Check

Most AI companies are not compliant and won't be by August 2. The Act's staggered timeline (different deadlines for different risk classes) was designed to prevent a bottleneck, but the high-risk and GPAI deadlines arrive before the implementing standards are fully published. Companies are flying blind on technical requirements.

The early enforcement strategy is expected to target the worst actors — companies deploying banned AI practices — while giving good-faith compliance efforts breathing room. But the uncertainty is itself costly.
