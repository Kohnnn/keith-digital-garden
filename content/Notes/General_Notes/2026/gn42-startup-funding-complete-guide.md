# Startup Funding: The Complete Guide

A synthesis of four videos — YC Startup School (Brad Flora), Rho's breakdown of the Kalanick story, a16z Speedrun on priced rounds, and the a16z partner roundtable on post-COVID startup building — into one comprehensive field guide.

---

## The Two Paths

Every founder makes a choice before they realise they are making it. There are two fundamentally different kinds of companies:

**The venture-scale company** raises massive rounds at high valuations and aims for acquisition or IPO. The investors writing the checks are betting on outlier returns: 10x, 50x, 300x. Their return only works if the company becomes huge. Everything about how venture works — board structure, liquidation preferences, protective provisions, timeline — is built around chasing that outcome.

**The durable profitable company** (formerly called a "lifestyle business") makes money, pays the founders well, and grows steadily for years. No outside investors needed because the company doesn't spend more than it makes. The founder owns most of it forever. That is the whole point.

Both are valid. The mistake founders make is wanting to build a profitable business they own while taking money from investors who only get paid if the company sells or goes public. You cannot take a $5M check from a fund built around outlier returns and then decide later you would rather run a profitable business at your own pace. By signing for that money, you are agreeing to grow fast enough to exit on the investors' timeline.

**What changed in 2025-2026:** AI collapsed the cost of building software. Products that used to require 10 engineers and $1M in seed money now get built by one person with AI tools. Solo-founded companies went from roughly a quarter of new startups on Carta in 2019 to over a third today. Skipping funding entirely is more realistic in 2026 than it has ever been.

## How the Rounds Actually Work

Funding comes in stages. No investor wants to fund years of a company they have not seen progress from.

### Pre-Seed
Idea stage, maybe a prototype. In 2026 this splits into two worlds. Many rounds are still under $250K. Larger ones (at least $1M) now average closer to $1.4M (Carta). Most pre-seed deals are SAFEs or convertible notes — you take money now and figure out valuation later with a cap between $10M and $15M. AI deals can push higher, but the average pre-seed founder is not raising at fantasy valuations just because AI is in the deck.

### Seed
Working product, early users, signs of PMF. Median seed post-money valuations hit a record $24M in late 2025 (Carta), up from ~$18M the year before. AI startups get a premium: Carta puts AI Series A valuations roughly 38% higher than non-AI, and the gap widens at later rounds. The best AI seed deals can clear $40M valuation, but the average company is not raising those numbers.

### Series A
The old rule of thumb was $1M ARR. That number is no longer enough. Most investors now want $2-3M ARR before they take you seriously — unless your growth rate is exceptional, your technical team is elite, or you have an AI angle investors are competing to get into. Each round has different rules. A pre-seed pitch is about who you are. A Series A pitch is about revenue, growth, and burn. Pitch the wrong stage and you are done.

### Series B+
Revenue in the tens of millions. By Series B, median founding team ownership is ~23% (Carta). The board can fire the founder as CEO. The founder needs board approval for everything from giving themselves a raise to making any major decision. The further along you go, the less it is about your story and the more it is about the numbers on your dashboard.

## The Myth-Busting Playbook (YC Brad Flora)

Brad Flora, YC Group Partner and former founder of Perfect Audience, walked through seven fundraising myths:

**Myth 1: Fundraising is glamorous.** Reality: it is coffee chats, not Shark Tank. One YC company raised $1.6M by meeting 160 investors over 4 months and 18 days. 39 said yes. Checks ranged from $5K to $200K. Fundraising is a grind — a series of one-on-one Zoom meetings while you try to collect checks.

**Myth 2: You need money before you can start.** Reality: the best founders build the first version first, even a toy version, get some users, then raise. When you have product and users, you have leverage. Investors want to jump on trains that are already moving.

**Case study — Solugen (YC W17):** Chemical manufacturing. Most founders would ask for $20M for a plant. Solugen built a tiny reactor that fit on a desk. Then a slightly larger version that made hydrogen peroxide to sell. During the YC batch they made $10K/month selling to hot tub supply stores — not huge, but proof of concept. They raised $4M seed because they had a working product with real customers. Today they have raised $400M+ and operate a full-scale plant.

**Myth 3: Your startup needs to be impressive.** Reality: most startups seem terrible at first and the best ones seem the most terrible. Airbnb was "a marketplace for renting an air mattress on someone's floor." DoorDash was "food delivery for the suburbs." OpenSea was "a marketplace for selling collectibles that only exist on your computer." You do not need to impress investors — you need to convince them. Those are different things.

**Myth 4: A high valuation means you won.** Reality: valuation gets the press release. Terms decide who controls the company. See the term sheet section below.

## Dilution Mechanics

This is the single most misunderstood concept in startup funding.

**How it works:** You and a co-founder start with 100 shares, 50 each. You each own 50%. An investor puts in $2M. The company creates 20 brand-new shares and gives those to the investor. Now there are 120 shares total. You still have 50 shares, but 50 out of 120 is 41.7%. You went from owning half the company to owning 41.7% of it without selling a single share. Your share count did not change. Your percentage did. That is dilution. And it happens every time the company raises a new round.

**The Facebook story:** Peter Thiel gave Zuckerberg and Saverin $500K for ~10% of the company in 2004, valuing Facebook at under $5M. The founders did not sell their own shares — Facebook created new shares for Peter, and everyone else's percentage shrunk. By IPO, every original founder owned a fraction of what they started with on paper. But the company was worth $100B, so that fraction was worth tens of billions. That is the deal: you give up percentage for the chance to build something much bigger than you could fund alone.

**The key formulas:**
- Dilution = Raise Amount / Post-Money Valuation
- Post-Money = Pre-Money + Raise Amount
- Post-Money = Raise Amount / Dilution

Example: raise a $3M seed at 20% dilution. Post-money = $3M / 0.20 = $15M. Pre-money = $15M - $3M = $12M. Price per share = Pre-money / existing shares.

**The cumulative effect:**

| Round | Amount | Post-money | Founder ownership |
|-------|--------|-----------|----------------|
| Seed | $3M | $15M | ~80% |
| Series A | $10M | $50M | ~64% |
| Series B | $25M | $150M | ~53% |
| Series C | $50M | $400M | ~46% |
| IPO + option pool | — | — | ~23% median |

**The Kalanick lesson:** By the time Uber went public, Travis Kalanick reportedly owned less than 9%. Two years earlier, he resigned as CEO after major investors demanded a leadership change. He built the company. The investors he had taken money from years earlier ended up with a meaningful say in who ran it.

## Term Sheets — What Matters Beyond Valuation

Founders fixate on valuation. It is the number in the press release, the one compared with other founders in their batch, the one celebrated when the round closes. At early stages, it is also the number that matters the least. Pre-seed and seed valuations are not calculated — they are negotiated. If five investors compete for the round, valuation goes up. If one investor is mildly interested, it does not. That is almost all of what determines an early-stage valuation.

But the headline valuation tells you nothing about who controls the company. There are four critical terms:

**1. Liquidation Preference.** Decides who gets paid first if the company sells. Founder-friendly: 1x non-participating (investors get their money back, nothing more). Dangerous: participating preferred (investors get their money back, then take their ownership % of whatever is left over). If you raised $10M and sold for $30M, participating means investors take $10M back, then take their % of the remaining $20M on top. The founder ends up with much less than expected.

**2. Board Structure.** By Series B, the founder often does not fully control the company. The board can fire the CEO. The founder needs board approval for major decisions. Kalanick built Uber. The investors ended up with a meaningful say in who ran it. Board seats matter when the company hits a crisis.

**3. Anti-Dilution Provisions.** Full ratchet: if the company raises at a lower valuation later, earlier investors get their price adjusted down. This can destroy founder ownership in a down round. Weighted average: a gentler adjustment. Full ratchet is aggressive and uncommon in standard deals.

**4. Protective Provisions.** Clauses giving investors veto power over big decisions: selling the company, raising another round, changing the budget, issuing new shares, taking on debt.

The general rule: competitive rounds produce the cleanest terms. Rounds with one investor tend to have more protection-heavy terms. Experienced founders do not ask "what valuation did I get?" They ask "what did I give up to get it?"

## Valuations — How They Are Really Set (a16z Speedrun)

In a priced round, the valuation is specified as pre-money or post-money. Pre-money is what current shareholders own. Post-money is company value after the new money is added. Basic formula: Post-money = Pre-money + Raise Amount. Price per share = Pre-money / pre-existing shares.

**Normal parameters:**
- Target runway: 18-24 months between rounds
- Typical dilution per round: 15-25%
- Seed round size: $2-5M
- Series A round size: $8-15M

The lead investor sets and negotiates the valuation and key terms. They usually write the largest check and set the pace. Followers join after the lead is established and accept the terms already negotiated. Strategy: secure a lead first, then bring in followers.

**The biggest trap:** Chasing a high headline valuation with bad terms. A $25M valuation with participating preferred, full ratchet anti-dilution, and an investor-controlled board is a worse deal than an $18M valuation with clean 1x non-participating, weighted average anti-dilution, and a founder-controlled board.

## The Post-COVID Environment (a16z Partners)

Ben Horowitz and Marc Andreessen covered several major shifts affecting how startups should think about building and fundraising:

**Trust in Institutions.** COVID was a massive blow to societal trust. The Gallup trust survey has been declining across most major institutions since the 1970s, but accelerated sharply post-2020. People on both sides of the lockdown debate view what happened as a fundamental breach of trust. The result: people are hungry for alternatives to existing institutions. This is structurally bullish for startups building new ones.

**Remote Work.** Video conferencing was a failed product for 55 years (AT&T Picturephone, 1965). The chicken-and-egg problem was solved overnight in 2020. Remote and hybrid work is here to stay. The worst configuration is hybrid — some in a room, some on video. Better to commit fully to one modality. This changes both how startups build teams and how investors evaluate them.

**AI Cost Collapse.** The biggest shift for founders: AI collapsed the cost of building software. Products needing 10 engineers and expensive infrastructure now take far less. Solo founders went from ~25% of new startups to over a third. This directly changes fundraising strategy: less capital needed means more leverage in negotiations and a credible option to skip funding entirely.

**The Operation Warp Speed Model.** The government-as-customer model (build this and we will buy it at scale) worked for vaccines. It could extend to chips, clean energy, biotech. Political will has stalled, but the precedent is real and could reshape how deep-tech startups fund their early stages.

## The Practical Playbook

### Before you raise
- Build something first. Even a toy version. Get users. Traction beats decks.
- Know which path you are on — venture-scale or durable profitable. Do not take venture money if you want to run a steady profitable business.
- Understand the instrument. SAFEs (92% of pre-seed deals) defer valuation to the next round. Priced rounds set valuation now.

### During fundraising
- Expect to meet 50-150 investors over 3-5 months
- Budget for ~25% meeting-to-check conversion
- Accept checks from $5K to $200K — they all count
- Track runway weekly, not quarterly
- Negotiate terms, not just valuation. Liquidation preference and board structure matter more than the headline number
- Understand what you are giving up for every term. Terms are written for the bad moments — the down round, the bridge round, the missed milestones

### The runway math
In 2022, founders were told 18 months. Fundraising takes longer now. The average company raising Series A in late 2024 waited 2.1 years since their previous round. Many founders target 24-30 months. If you are not hitting Series A revenue expectations by month 12-14, uncomfortable conversations start.

### The bridge trap
When growth slows and cash runs low before the next milestone, founders scramble for a bridge round — emergency funding that often comes at lower valuation, higher dilution, and worse terms. This is the most common way venture-backed startups die: running out of money between rounds. The spending that kills you rarely happens all at once. It creeps up a few thousand dollars at a time.

### Key data points

| Metric | Value |
|--------|-------|
| Runway target | 18-30 months |
| Dilution per round | 15-25% |
| Median seed post-money (2026) | ~$24M |
| AI valuation premium | ~38% higher than non-AI |
| Series A ARR threshold | $2-3M (was $1M) |
| Founder ownership at Series B | ~23% median (Carta) |
| Pre-seed SAFE usage | ~92% of rounds |
| Meeting conversion rate | ~25% success |

## Red Flags in Term Sheets

- Participating liquidation preference (founders should almost never accept this at early stage)
- Full ratchet anti-dilution
- Investor-controlled board at Seed
- Raising before you have any users or traction
- Headline valuation that sounds great but hides bad terms
- Not understanding what happens in the downside scenarios

---

## The Bottom Line

The 2026 fundraising landscape is more uneven than ever. For AI companies with elite teams and traction, capital is abundant and terms are clean. For regular software companies with steady growth, the bar is higher and the process takes longer.

But the most important change is that not raising at all is a credible option now in a way it has never been before. AI-driven solo founders and small teams can build real businesses with real revenue on dramatically less capital.

The founders who understand the full picture — the rounds, the dilution, the terms, the clock — are the ones who walk out with the company they actually wanted to build.
