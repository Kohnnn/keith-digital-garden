Madrid tripled its metro system from 71 to 197 miles (114 to 317 km) between 1995 and 2007 — faster and cheaper than almost any other city in the world. The 35-mile 1995-1999 expansion cost ~$2.8 billion. New York's 1.5-mile Hudson Yards extension cost about the same. London's Jubilee Line Extension cost 10x per mile.

This is one of the best Works in Progress deep-dives I've read. Four lessons that apply well beyond transit:

## 1. City-level ownership

The Community of Madrid concentrated planning, funding, and construction power at the right level. The regional president could make an electoral promise to build metro, had the tax/spending authority to fund it, and was personally accountable for delivery. No finger-pointing between transit authorities and national departments. Compare this to London or New York where projects get stuck between local, regional, and national bodies for years.

## 2. Time is actually money

Madrid ran tunnel boring machines 24/7. Environmental impact assessments for a 4-mile extension were 19 pages. The Portishead branch line in England had a 17,912-page environmental statement for 3.3 miles. Madrid's streamlined approach meant less exposure to inflation, political changes, and contractor churn. Building fast was the cost-saving move, not the expensive one.

## 3. Standardize everything

Madrid used copy-and-paste station designs. London's Jubilee Line had a different starchitect per station. Madrid's stations are functional, easy to navigate, and character comes from murals and sculpture — not architectural megastructures. The design philosophy was "focused on the needs of users, rather than architectural beauty or exotic materials." They also chose fixed-block signaling (tried-and-tested) over moving-block (complex, expensive, delays). London's Elizabeth line was finished constructing in 2019 but didn't open until 2023 because of signaling complexity. Madrid's window between construction end and opening: three weeks.

## 4. Invest in state capacity

Madrid hired a small, well-paid in-house engineering team that stayed through all projects. They created Mintra, a public company that could borrow off-books and pay above government scales to retain talent. Procurement weighted technical merit at 50%, timeline at 20%, and cost at only 30%. In the UK/US, lowest-cost bidding is the norm, which incentivizes contractors to under-bid and then raise costs via change orders.

## My take

The Madrid case destroys the "it's expensive because we're building in a democratic society" excuse. Madrid was democratic, had rigorous public consultation (just streamlined), and built 10x cheaper. The real obstacle is institutional fragmentation — too many veto points, diffused accountability, and a risk-averse culture that favors 17,000-page environmental statements over actually building things. The metro is so popular today that 75% of riders are "very satisfied" and it carries 210 trips per resident per year (London: 155, NYC: 203).
