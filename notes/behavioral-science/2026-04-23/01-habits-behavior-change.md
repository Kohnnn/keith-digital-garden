## 1. The Fogg Behavior Model (B = M × A × P)

### 1.1 The Core Equation

B.J. Fogg, director of Stanford's Behavior Design Lab, articulated behavior as a mathematical function:

```
Behavior = Motivation × Ability × Prompt
```

All three factors must converge simultaneously for behavior to occur. Remove any one, and the behavior fails.

| Element | High State | Low State |
|---------|-----------|-----------|
| **Motivation** | Excited, pain-avoiding | Indifferent, procrastinating |
| **Ability** | Skillful, resources available | Complex, costly |
| **Prompt** | Visible trigger present | Absent or ignored |

### 1.2 Motivation Triad

Fogg identifies three motivators that drive behavior:

```
PAIN ← → PLEASURE
HOPE ← → FEAR
SOCIAL ACCEPTANCE ← → SOCIAL REJECTION
```

These are the emotional engines that power behavior. Interventions targeting these motivators see higher engagement rates.

### 1.3 Ability: Making Things Easy

Fogg emphasizes that **ability is more malleable than motivation**. Instead of pushing people to work harder, design for simplicity:

| Strategy | Example |
|----------|---------|
| **Skill** | Tutorial before use |
| **Time** | Reduce duration required |
| **Money** | Reduce cost barrier |
| **Physical Effort** | Ergonomic design |
| **Brain Cycles** | Simplify decision-making |
| **Social Deviance** | Normalize the behavior |
| **Non-Routine** | Anchor to existing habits |

### 1.4 Prompt Design

Prompts are the trigger that initiates action. Fogg classifies them:

- **Facilitator Prompt**: When motivation is high, ability is low (e.g., "Just do it!" fails)
- **Spark Prompt**: When ability is high, motivation is low (e.g., motivational nudge)
- **Signal Prompt**: When both motivation and ability are adequate (e.g., reminder notification)

## 2. Tiny Habits Method

### 2.1 Shrink to Grow

Fogg's Tiny Habits methodology inverts traditional behavior change:

> "Make it so small you can't say no."

Instead of "Exercise 30 minutes," anchor to: "After I pee, do 1 pushup."

### 2.2 The Anatomy of a Tiny Habit

```
ANCHOR + TINY BEHAVIOR + IMMEDIATE CELEBRATION

Example: After I sit down [ANCHOR], 
         I will put one book on my lap [TINY], 
         and smile [CELEBRATION].
```

### 2.3 Three Steps to Tiny Habits

1. **Anchor moment** — An existing routine or event
2. **New tiny behavior** — Takes <30 seconds, no motivation required
3. **Celebration** — Creates positive emotion, reinforces neural pathway

## 3. James Clear's Four Laws of Behavior Change

### 3.1 Overview

Clear distills behavior change into a memorable framework:

| Law | Focus | Inverse |
|-----|-------|---------|
| **1. Make It Obvious** | Environment design | Make It Invisible |
| **2. Make It Attractive** | Dopamine anticipation | Make It Unattractive |
| **3. Make It Easy** | Friction reduction | Make It Difficult |
| **4. Make It Satisfying** | Reward reinforcement | Make It Unsatisfying |

### 3.2 Law 1: Make It Obvious

**Environment is the invisible architect of behavior.**

```
[INPUT] → Environment → [OUTPUT]
```

**Implementation:**
- **Habit Stacking**: "After I [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT]"
- **Design your environment**: Place cues in plain sight
- **Remove temptation**: Out of sight = out of mind

**The Two-Minute Rule**: When starting a new habit, it should take less than two minutes.

### 3.3 Law 2: Make It Attractive

**Temptation Bundling** links desired behavior with enjoyable activity:

```
[NEEDED HABIT] + [WANTED ACTIVITY] = ATTRACTIVE PACKAGE
```

**Example:** "Only watch Netflix while doing squats."

**Social Dopamine:** We are tribal. We imitate:
1. The close (friends/family)
2. The many (social norms)
3. The powerful (status/prestige)

### 3.4 Law 3: Make It Easy

**The Law of Least Effort:** Reduce friction for good habits, increase friction for bad ones.

```
FRICTION DIAGRAM:

Good Habit + Friction → Abandoned
Bad Habit + Friction → Abandoned

Good Habit - Friction → Automatic
Bad Habit - Friction → Automatic
```

**Prime Environment for Success:**
- Prepare your environment for the next action
- One decision = one action (no ambiguity)
- Master habits before optimizing

### 3.5 Law 4: Make It Satisfying

**The Cardinal Rule of Behavior Change:**

> What is immediately rewarded is repeated. What is immediately punished is avoided.

**Track your habits:** Never break the chain twice. Visual streaks create identity.

**Don't break the chain:**

| Day | Mon | Tue | Wed | Thu | Fri | Sat | Sun |
|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|-----|
| Status | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |

One miss is an accident. Two misses is a new habit.

## 4. Charles Duhigg — The Habit Loop

### 4.1 The Neurological Foundation

Habits form in the **basal ganglia** — the same region responsible for emotions, memories, and pattern recognition. This explains why habits are so automatic yet emotionally charged.

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    HABIT LOOP                            │
│                                                          │
│   ┌──────┐     ┌─────────┐     ┌───────┐                 │
│   │ CUE  │ ──→ │ ROUTINE │ ──→ │REWARD│                 │
│   └──────┘     └─────────┘     └───────┘                 │
│       ↑                                                   │
│       └────────── CRAVING ───────────────────────────────│
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 4.2 Cue: The Trigger

**The Five Categories of Cues:**

| Category | Example |
|----------|---------|
| **Location** | Specific room, building, desk |
| **Time** | 9 AM, after lunch, before bed |
| **Emotional State** | Bored, stressed, anxious |
| **Preceding Event** | Walking through door, finishing call |
| **Other People** | Colleague enters, friend texts |

### 4.3 Routine: The Behavior

The routine is what we most visibly change. It can be:
- Physical (smoking, exercising)
- Mental (second-guessing, procrastinating)
- Emotional (worrying, celebrating)

### 4.4 Reward: The Satisfaction

Rewards satisfy cravings and teach the brain which patterns are worth encoding. The brain continuously evaluates: "Is this worth remembering?"

### 4.5 Craving: The Engine

**The Missing Link:** Before Duhigg's later refinements, craving was the underdeveloped element.

> Craving is the anticipation of the reward, not the reward itself.

Without craving, the loop doesn't have momentum.

## 5. The Golden Rule of Habit Change

### 5.1 Why Willpower Alone Fails

Willpower is like a muscle — it fatigues. Relying on willpower to resist bad habits leads to decision fatigue and eventual relapse.

### 5.2 The Four-Step Framework

| Step | Question | Action |
|------|----------|--------|
| 1 | Identify the routine | What is the observable behavior? |
| 2 | Experiment with rewards | What need does this satisfy? |
| 3 | Isolate the cue | What triggers the routine? |
| 4 | Have a plan | What will you do instead? |

### 5.3 Substitution, Not Elimination

**Never say "stop."** Replace the routine with a healthier alternative that satisfies the same craving.

**Example — Caffeine Cravings:**
- Wake up → Coffee (routine)
- What craving? → Alertness
- Substitute: Stretching → shower → green tea → coffee

## 6. Identity-Based Habits

### 6.1 The Three Layers of Change

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                 OUTCOME                          │
│     "I want to run a marathon"                   │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                 PROCESS                          │
│     "I want to become a runner"                  │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                 IDENTITY                         │
│     "I am a runner"                              │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

The most effective change starts from identity, not outcomes.

### 6.2 Two Forms of Identity

| Type | Focus | Example |
|------|-------|---------|
| **Belief Identity** | What you believe about yourself | "I'm the type who exercises" |
| **Evidence Identity** | What you do that proves it | Running 3x/week |

Evidence identity trumps belief. **You are what you repeatedly do.**

## 7. Implementation Intention

### 7.1 The Power of "When-Then"

Gollwitzer's research shows that specific implementation intentions dramatically increase follow-through:

```
"When [CUE], I will [ACTION]"

Example: "When I sit on the bus (NOT "When I have time"), 
         I will read one chapter."
```

### 7.2 The Must-Achieve Effect

Implementation intentions create a **must-achieve** mindset rather than a **wish-and-see** approach.

| Type | Format | Success Rate |
|------|--------|--------------|
| Goal intention | "I will try to exercise" | ~35% |
| Implementation intention | "When it's 7am, I will go to the gym" | ~65% |

```

```

### 8.2 Stacking Templates

## 9. Advanced: Habit Contract & Accountability

### 9.1 Social Accountability

Habits stick better with social stakes:

- **Habit Contract**: Written agreement with consequences
- **Habit Partner**: Accountability buddy who checks in
- **Public Commitment**: Social media declarations

### 9.2 Bad Habit Architecture

**First, add friction:**

```
Cue → [ADD Friction] → Routine → Reward
```

**Second, replace the routine:**

```
Cue → [Same Friction] → New Routine → Reward
```

**Third, eliminate the cue:**

```
[Cue Removed] → No Routine → No Reward
```

## 10. The Role of Stress in Habit Formation

### 10.1 Stress Hijacks Rationality

Under stress, the prefrontal cortex (executive function) goes offline, and the basal ganglia (habit center) takes over.

**The Stress Loop:**

```
Stress → Cue → Old Habit → Temporary Relief → More Stress
```

### 10.2 Habit Reset Under Stress

**New habits are fragile during stress.** This is why relapse often occurs during:
- Job changes
- Relationship shifts
- Travel
- Illness

**Prevention:** Pre-commit to a "stress protocol" before stress arrives.
