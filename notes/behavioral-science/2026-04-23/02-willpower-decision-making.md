## 1. Roy Baumeister — Ego Depletion

### 1.1 The Ego Depletion Theory

Baumeister's landmark research introduced the concept of **ego depletion** — the idea that self-control and willpower draw from a limited mental resource that can be exhausted.

```
EGO DEPLETION MODEL:

Self-Control Task → Glucose Consumption → Reduced Capacity → 
→ Subsequent Tasks Impaired

High Demands ──────────────────────────────→ Depletion
         ↓
    Performance Declines
```

### 1.2 The Original Experiments

Baumeister's foundational studies used elegant designs:

**Study 1: The Radish Study**
- Participants: Three groups
  - Group A: Ate only radishes (resisting cookies)
  - Group B: Ate only cookies
  - Group C: Nothing (control)
- Task: Solve impossible puzzles
- Result: Radish group quit fastest (effortful restraint = depleted)

**Study 2: The Letter Task**
- Task 1: Don't use certain letters in writing
- Task 2: Solve puzzles requiring self-control
- Result: Those who restricted letters performed worse on puzzles

### 1.3 The Glucose Hypothesis

Baumeister proposed that glucose is the substrate for willpower:

| Finding | Implication |
|---------|-------------|
| Low blood glucose = impaired self-control | Diet affects discipline |
| Glucose restores depleted self-control | Sugar can temporarily help |
| Rationing glucose impairs all self-control | One task depletes others |

### 1.4 Later Challenges & Refinements

Recent replication debates have refined the ego depletion model:

**Critiques:**
- Some replication studies failed to confirm glucose effects
- Effect sizes may be smaller than originally claimed
- Context and individual differences matter more

**Refinements:**
- **Self-control strength model**: Rather than depletion, self-control uses energy
- **Motivational factors**: Belief in limits can create limits
- **Rest and recovery**: Sleep and breaks restore capacity

## 2. Decision Fatigue & Choice Overload

### 2.1 The Parcimony Effect

Baumeister's later work connected ego depletion to decision-making:

```
DECISION FATIGUE CASCADE:

Early in Day                    Late in Day
────────────                    ────────────
High prefrontal activity    →   Reduced prefrontal activity
Good decisions               →   Decision avoidance / shortcuts
Clear priorities             →   Defaults and shortcuts

Timeline:
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ Morning (9-11am)  │  Afternoon (1-3pm)  │  Evening (6-8pm)   │
│     Peak          │      Dip           │     Recovery       │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 2.2 The Judicial Parole Study

Danziger, Levav & Avnaim-Pesso (2011) demonstrated decision fatigue in Israeli parole courts:

**Key Findings:**
- Judges reviewed cases sequentially
- Favorable rulings: 65% at start → 0% before lunch → 65% after lunch
- Sugar didn't help — what helped was a break

| Time of Day | Favorable Ruling Rate |
|-------------|----------------------|
| After food break | 65% |
| Morning sessions | 50% |
| Before lunch | 20% |
| Just before break | 0% |

### 2.3 Implications for Productivity

**Prescription: Protect decision-making for high-stakes choices**

| Strategy | Why It Works |
|----------|-------------|
| Handle email at set times | Prevents decision drain |
| Make important calls in morning | Peak capacity |
| Meal breaks between decisions | Glucose restoration |
| Pre-commit to routines | Reduces daily decisions |
| Default options | Sidesteps fatigue |

## 3. Daniel Kahneman — Dual-Process Theory

### 3.1 Two Systems of Thinking

Kahneman's framework identifies two cognitive modes:

```
┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    SYSTEM 1: FAST                           │
│                       "The Animal"                           │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ • Automatic, effortless                                    │
│ • Emotional, intuitive                                      │
│ • Heuristic-based                                           │
│ • Runs continuously                                         │
│ • Cannot be turned off                                      │
│ • Examples: Recognizing emotions, driving familiar route   │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    SYSTEM 2: SLOW                            │
│                       "The Rationalist"                      │
├──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│ • Deliberate, effortful                                    │
│ • Logical, analytical                                      │
│ • Rule-based                                               │
│ • Activated when needed                                    │
│ • Lazy — defaults to System 1 shortcuts                     │
│ • Examples: Calculating, learning new skills, proofreading  │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 3.2 System 1 Characteristics

**What System 1 Does Well:**

| Function | Example |
|----------|---------|
| Detecting anomalies | "That price seems too high" |
| Recognizing people | Face identification |
| Emotional responses | Fear when seeing a snake |
| Priming effects | Exposure influences preference |
| Anchoring | First number influences estimate |
| Availability heuristic | Recent events seem more likely |

**System 1 Biases:**

| Bias | Definition | Example |
|------|------------|---------|
| Confirmation bias | Seek info confirming beliefs | Ignore contradictory evidence |
| Halo effect | Good impression → good attributes | Attractive = competent |
| Anchoring | First info dominates | Price negotiation |
| Sunk cost | Past investment drives decisions | "We already paid" |
| Optimism bias | Underestimate risks | "It won't happen to me" |

### 3.3 System 2 Characteristics

**When to Engage System 2:**

- Making important decisions
- Learning new skills
- Analyzing data
- Planning long-term
- Evaluating claims
- Controlling impulses

**System 2 Limitations:**

| Limitation | Consequence |
|------------|-------------|
| **Lazy** | Defaults to System 1 shortcuts |
| **Fatigued** | Quality degrades with use |
| **Limited capacity** | Only handles ~7 chunks |
| **Ego depletion vulnerability** | Self-control requires System 2 |

## 4. Cognitive Biases & Heuristics

### 4.1 The Heuristics Framework

Tversky & Kahneman (1974) identified three foundational heuristics:

```
HEURISTICS & BIASES MODEL:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    HEURISTICS                                │
│  (Mental Shortcuts that usually work, sometimes fail)        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│                                                              │

│         │                │                │                  │
│    "Does it        "Is it easy      "First number         │
│    look like X?"   to recall?"       dominates?"          │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 4.2 Representativeness Heuristic

**Rule:** "Does it look like a typical X?"

**Biases:**
- Ignoring base rates
- The conjunction fallacy (Linda problem)
- Overlooking sample size

**Example:**
```
Question: "Linda is 31, single, outspoken, and 
          majored in philosophy. What is more likely?"

A) Linda is a bank teller
B) Linda is a bank teller AND active in feminist movement

System 1 says: B (represents "feminist profile")
Statistics say: A (conjunction is always less probable)
```

### 4.3 Availability Heuristic

**Rule:** "Easy to recall = common"

**Biases:**
- Media overrepresentation of dramatic events
- Recent events overweighted
- Vivid memories overcounted

**Example:**
- People fear sharks more than cars (media coverage)
- Plane crashes seem more common than car accidents

### 4.4 Anchoring Effect

**Rule:** First number becomes a reference point

**Research:**
- Restaurant menus with high-priced items → everything seems cheaper
- Arbitrary random numbers influence estimates
- Negotiation starts with anchored figure

**Experiment:**
```
Anchor: "Is Gandhi older or younger than 144?" → Estimate ~70
Anchor: "Is Gandhi older or younger than 35?" → Estimate ~50
Actual: 78
```

## 5. Prospect Theory

### 5.1 The Core Insight

Kahneman & Tversky's Prospect Theory explains irrational economic choices:

```
PROSPECT THEORY VALUE FUNCTION:

    GAINS                    LOSSES
       │                        │
   +15 │              ╱╱       │      ╱╱
       │            ╱╱          │    ╱╱
    +5 │          ╱             │  ╱╱
       │        ╱                │╱
   ────┼──────╱────────────────┼────────────────
       │    ╱                   │
  -5   │  ╱                     │
       │╱                       │
  -15  │                        │
       └────────────────────────┘
       ↑                ↑
    Concave          Convex
    (Risk-averse)    (Risk-seeking)
```

### 5.2 Four Effects of Prospect Theory

| Effect | Description | Example |
|--------|-------------|---------|
| **Loss aversion** | Losses hurt ~2x more than gains feel good | "Don't take my parking spot!" |
| **Diminishing sensitivity** | Marginal changes matter less | $100 means more when broke |
| **Reflection** | Risk-seeking for losses, risk-averse for gains | Gambling to avoid locking in loss |
| **Fourfold pattern** | Asymmetric risk preferences | High probability: avoid; Low probability: gamble |

### 5.3 Practical Implications

**Investment Decisions:**
- Labeling matters ("lock in gains" vs "avoid locking in losses")
- Loss aversion causes portfolio paralysis
- Reference points shift over time

**Negotiation:**
- Opening position anchors the range
- Reference point determines perceived fairness
- Loss framing motivates action

## 6. The Planning Fallacy

### 6.1 Why Plans Fail

Kahneman identified the **planning fallacy** — the tendency to underestimate time, costs, and risks while overestimating benefits.

**Characteristics:**
- Only see own perspective (inside view)
- Ignore base rates from similar projects
- Assume things will go smoothly
- Discount past failures

### 6.2 The Outside View

**The Cure:** Take the base rate from comparable cases.

```
PLANNING FALLACY vs OUTSIDE VIEW:

Planning Fallacy (Inside View):
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ "Our project is unique, we have good team,     │
│  strong leadership, so we'll beat the odds."   │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Outside View:
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ "Projects of this type average 70% overrun.     │
│  What are the concrete reasons we won't?"      │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

### 6.3 Pre-Mortem Analysis

Kahneman's antidote: Imagine the project failed catastrophically, then work backward.

**Pre-Mortem Protocol:**
1. Assume project failed
2. Generate reasons why (team brainstorm)
3. Identify mitigation strategies
4. Build those into the plan

## 7. Anchoring & Adjustment Heuristic

### 7.1 How Anchoring Works

Initial exposure to a number creates a reference point that subsequent judgments adjust from — even when the anchor is arbitrary.

**Mechanism:**

```
ARBITRARY COHERENCE MODEL:

Arbitrary Anchor → Automatic Adjustment → Final Estimate
     ↓
    Even random numbers create lasting effects
    Once anchored, adjustments are insufficient
```

### 7.2 Anchoring in Practice

| Domain | How Anchoring Manifests |
|--------|------------------------|
| **Salary negotiations** | First offer sets the range |
| **Real estate** | Listed price influences offers |
| **Pricing** | MSRP despite discounts |
| **Estimates** | Expert judgment anchored by initial value |

### 7.3 Overcoming Anchoring

**For Decision-Makers:**
- Consider alternative anchors
- Use the outside view
- Pre-commit to fair value before negotiation
- Ask "What other anchor might apply?"

## 8. Cognitive Load & Decision Quality

### 8.1 Load Effects

Both ego depletion and cognitive load impair System 2 function:

```
COGNITIVE LOAD MODEL:

Low Load (System 2 available):
→ Analytical thinking
→ Self-control intact
→ Good judgment

High Load (System 2 unavailable):
→ Reliance on System 1 shortcuts
→ Reduced self-control
→ Heuristic-driven decisions
```

### 8.2 Sources of Cognitive Load

| Source | Impact |
|--------|--------|
| Decision complexity | Multiple options = mental strain |
| Information overload | Analysis paralysis |
| Emotional stress | Drains executive resources |
| Sleep deprivation | Reduces prefrontal function |
| Physical fatigue | Impairs concentration |

### 8.3 Load Management Strategies

**Structural:**
- Reduce unnecessary decisions
- Create decision rules/routines
- Batch similar decisions
- Limit information sources

**Environmental:**
- Quiet spaces for important choices
- Time-boxed decision windows
- Pre-decision commitments

## 9. The Peak-End Rule

### 9.1 Memory vs Experience

Kahneman discovered that humans don't experience time uniformly:

**The Peak-End Rule:**
```
Experience Profile:   ┌─────┐
                      │peak │
                      │     └───┐
                      │        │end
                      └────────┴──
                      
Memory formation: Peaks and endings dominate memory
                  Middle periods are forgotten
```

### 9.2 Implications

**For Experiences:**
- Two-minute painful ending ruins overall memory
- Peak intensity matters more than duration
- Positive endings can redeem difficult experiences

**For Design:**
- Medical procedures: Minimize peak pain and final discomfort
- Customer experience: End on positive note
- Presentations: Strong close over smooth middle

## 10. Applying Willpower Science

### 10.1 The Willpower Budget

Based on Baumeister's research, treat self-control as a depleting resource:

```
WEEKLY WILLPOWER BUDGET:

┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│                    MONDAY (Full Budget)                     │
│  Allocate to: Important decisions, difficult habits         │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
                         ↓ Depletes
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐

│  Allocate to: Routine tasks, default choices                │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Budget Restoration: Sleep, food breaks, rest days
```

### 10.2 Strategic Willpower Deployment

| Priority | When to Execute | Why |
|----------|----------------|-----|
| Major decisions | Morning | Fresh capacity |
| Habit formation | Consistent time | Builds routine |
| Difficult conversations | Post-lunch with breaks | Controlled environment |
| Routine tasks | Low-energy periods | Preserve capacity |

### 10.3 Protecting Your Willpower

**Recovery:**
- Sleep 7-9 hours (prefrontal restoration)
- Eat regular meals (glucose maintenance)
- Take breaks between demands
- Exercise regularly (surprisingly restores capacity)

**Prevention:**
- Reduce unnecessary decisions
- Pre-commit to routines
- Remove temptations (not just resist them)
- Stack habits to reduce willpower demands

## 12. Key Takeaways

1. **Willpower is finite** — treat it as a depleting resource
2. **Decision fatigue is real** — protect high-stakes choices for peak times
3. **Two systems operate** — System 1 is fast but biased, System 2 is slow but effortful
4. **Anchors influence everything** — be aware of arbitrary reference points
5. **Losses hurt more than gains feel good** — loss aversion ~2x stronger
6. **Planning fallacy is universal** — use the outside view
7. **Protect your cognitive budget** — sleep, food, breaks restore capacity
8. **Pre-commit to overcome present bias** — bind your future self
